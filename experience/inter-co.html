The role

Division 10 covers the specialty items that go into a finished building, from washroom partitions and lockers to operable walls, signage and roof hatches. Each project started as an estimate and ended with installed product and a final bill. In between, I turned architectural drawings into shop drawings, planned around the general contractor's schedule, managed changes and disputes, and kept materials and crews arriving at the right place at the right time.

Partway through, I moved from Toronto to Winnipeg to run our projects there. Installers were scarce, so scheduling crews became the hardest part of the job. I also trained and mentored a junior project manager who reported to me.

How a project ran

  1. Review the estimate

    Take over the file from the estimator, clarify questions, and send it back if something big is missing, like a room with no note explaining why.

  2. Shop drawings

    Draft shop drawings from the specifications and architectural drawings, and work out lead times and install durations.

  3. Submit and revise

    Submit drawings with a request for when our scope is needed on site, review the returned notes, and raise change orders for any changes.

  4. Plan the schedule

    Build a project plan around the general contractor's timeline and recommend ways to handle long lead times.

  5. Measure the site

    Send installers to measure, review site conditions, update the drawings, and raise change orders for anything significant.

  6. Order and deliver

    Order materials once the general contractor approves, and plan how they get to site and installed.

  7. Install

    Schedule crews and handle the emergencies: weather, rejected deliveries that leave a crew idle, missing or damaged pieces, and confusion over where material goes.

  8. Close out

    Resolve deficiencies, bill as work is completed, and settle any invoicing disputes.

Cost analysis

Working out tariff exposure before an order shipped

Problem
A cross-border operable wall order was hit by new 25% tariffs, and the general contractor needed the real cost before it shipped.
What I did
Calculated GST from the total duty plus surtaxes, converted USD to CAD, and modelled the tariff on each of four panel assemblies. Flagged the two things most likely to move the number, the exchange rate and the final field dimensions, and sent the full calculation to the general contractor.
Result
About $26K of tariff exposure on roughly $104K of panels, laid out for the general contractor to confirm before the order went ahead.
Root cause

Finding the real reason six projects were delayed

Problem
Several jobs were being reported internally as delayed because "further clarification" was needed.
What I did
Went straight to the manufacturer's account contact and confirmed the real cause: a billing hold on our side, not a technical issue. Pushed back on the internal explanation instead of repeating it.
Result
One report to leadership listing all six affected projects with the true cause and a revised timeline for each.
Field QA

Catching a field change before panels were made

Problem
The general contractor raised the installed track height on site without telling us, which would have made the panels already on order too short.
What I did
Once the installer flagged it, stopped the order, had the openings remeasured, reissued the shop drawing, and raised a change order for the added material and labour.
Result
Wrong-size panels were never manufactured, and the cost of the unapproved change was put back on the change that caused it.
Fault isolation

Ruling out installer error on a door that arrived too small

Problem
A custom pocket door arrived too small for its opening, and it wasn't clear whose mistake it was.
What I did
Went back through the email trail and confirmed the installer's dimensions matched what had been ordered. Then sent the manufacturer a two-part report, describing the problem and the action requested, proposing a seal fix with a reorder and back charge as the fallback.
Result
The claim reached the manufacturer with the cause already established and a clear path either way.
Process control

Turning a delivery incident into a standing rule

Problem
A courier arranged an early delivery directly with someone on site, and 700-lb panels ended up outdoors in a storm.
What I did
When moving companies turned the job down, recognized it needed a rigging crew and escalated with that specific ask, keeping the general contractor and leadership updated until movers were booked. Afterwards, set a rule with the courier: no delivery changes without written approval from me.
Result
The panels were moved, and the gap that caused the incident was closed for future deliveries.
Close-out

Closing about 60 deficiencies before an access deadline

Problem
After a fixed date, any further work would need paid evening and weekend access with no help from the general contractor, and about 60 deficiencies were still open.
What I did
Chose low-cost fixes over replacements: suction cups to pull dents out of whiteboards, peel-and-stick repairs where that wasn't enough, and leftover material, like trim for gym markerboards, to close other items.
Result
The list was closed out against the deadline, mostly without ordering replacements.

What I learned

  • Find the real cause before reporting it. Six projects were being reported as delayed for "clarification". One call to the manufacturer showed it was a billing hold on our side, so leadership got the true picture and a timeline they could rely on.
  • Put the numbers in front of people before they have to ask. I sent the general contractor the full tariff calculation, including the two figures most likely to change, before the order shipped.
  • Turn incidents into rules. After 700-lb panels were left outside in a storm, I set a standing rule with the courier: no delivery changes without our written approval.
