The role

Method connects to a business's accounting software and can be customized to fit how that business runs. In Professional Services, clients bought blocks of hours, and I used them to build what they needed: automations, small apps, reports and customer portal changes. Many of my clients were on ongoing monthly plans, so I worked with the same owners over months and helped decide what to build next.

Much of the work was analysis. Requests often arrived as a single sentence, so I worked with the client to define the business rules behind them before building anything.

How a project ran

  1. Discovery

    Led a one to two hour discovery session to understand the business need, confirm what the platform could support, break the request into tasks, define the requirements for each screen, and identify impacts on related processes.

  2. Scope confirmation

    Sent a written summary with a timeline and hours estimate, secured the client's approval, scheduled the build time, and booked the next touchpoint: a demo or a weekly progress review.

  3. Solution design

    Reviewed the discovery notes, mapped the target workflow, and documented the build plan and technical specifications for each screen before development began.

  4. Build

    Configured the screens and business logic, drawing on Method's documentation and my teammates' expertise where needed, and logged the work completed in each session.

  5. Testing and review

    Tested the full workflow end to end and resolved defects, then demonstrated the solution to the client, answered questions and gathered feedback.

  6. Refinement and rollout

    Applied the final changes, released the solution to the appropriate users, and followed up on any issues at the next check-in.

  7. Documentation

    Produced a standard operating procedure and a recorded walkthrough for the client's internal library.

  8. Continuous improvement

    Scoped the next phase of work and recommended further enhancements, starting the cycle again.

Client A · accounting firm

Turning a one-line request into an automated overdue-estimate reminder

Problem
The firm chased overdue estimates by hand. The request was one line, and one rep could cover several companies, which Method's data model didn't link natively.
What I did
Pinned down the rules before building: what counts as overdue, who gets the email when one rep covers several companies, the table layout, how often it sends, and who is excluded. Then built a table linking companies to a rep, an exception list staff can maintain themselves, and a daily routine that groups overdue totals per company into one email and logs an activity.
Result
Manual follow-up replaced by a scheduled reminder with an audit trail.
Client B · general contractor (construction)

Time tracking with overtime and escalating reminders

Problem
Time entries had to be split into regular and overtime by hand, and some staff didn't log time at all.
What I did
Built logic that splits each time entry into regular, overtime and total, with weekly rollups for individuals and managers. Added three levels of follow-up: a reminder email, a pop-up once an entry is a week late, and a lockout that requires logging time before the main screen can be used. Also synced work order times to their linked activities and built individual and company-wide calendar views.
Result
Overtime calculated automatically and missing time entries followed up without anyone chasing them.
Client A · accounting firm

Replacing a spreadsheet with an app

Problem
Incoming and outgoing mail was tracked in a spreadsheet that one person maintained by hand.
What I did
Built an in-system app: a list and edit screen as flexible as the spreadsheet, attachments, reference lists (like couriers) that can grow over time, a settings screen for email templates, daily received and sent routines, an Excel import for staff without a login, and a tool that removes duplicates from that import.
Result
The spreadsheet retired in favour of one shared app with notifications built in.
Client C · storage company

Auditing a broken build and recommending a rebuild

Problem
A questionnaire app from a previous consultant had the same fields copied across 10+ screens, needed a new screen for every new questionnaire, and broke often.
What I did
Audited the app and documented the root cause. Shipped a stopgap, one reusable email routine any screen can call instead of ten separate ones, and formally recommended rebuilding instead of patching.
Result
An immediate fix plus a documented rebuild recommendation for the client to plan around.
Client D · furniture company

Letting the client manage their own purchase order statuses

Problem
A previous consultant had hardcoded every purchase order status, and the email that went with it, into a single action set of more than 110 lines. Adding or changing a status meant editing that logic directly.
What I did
Moved the statuses into a settings table the client's staff can edit themselves, and added a picker for the email template and CC list used by each status. The action set now reads from those settings instead of spelling out every case.
Result
The logic dropped to about 25 lines, roughly 75% smaller. The client can now add or change statuses without new development, and each status can send its own notification email, which the old version couldn't do.
Client E · consulting firm

Portal access for parent and sub-customers

Problem
Parent companies needed to see everything for their sub-customers in the customer portal, while each sub-customer should only see its own records.
What I did
Designed separate portal views by access level, a customer-facing activity view, and a self-service "request a change to my info" form that routes to the account's rep.
Result
Each customer sees exactly what they should, and change requests arrive in one place.

What I learned

  • Pin down the rules before building. A one-line request like "remind people about overdue estimates" hid five decisions: what counts as overdue, who gets the email, how often it sends, who is excluded, and what happens when one rep covers several companies. Answering those first saved rework later.
  • Sometimes the honest answer is "rebuild". On an app inherited from a previous consultant, a quick stopgap plus a written case for rebuilding helped the client more than another patch would have.
  • Give users the controls instead of hardcoding them. Moving purchase order statuses into a settings table cut 110+ lines to about 25, and the client can now change statuses without calling us.
