Returns App for a Food Distributor

Replacing a paper returns process with a Power Apps app for a wholesale food distributor, as project manager of a six-person capstone team.

ClientToronto Sun Wah Trading, a Toronto food service distributor
ToolsMicrosoft Power Apps, Power Automate, SharePoint

Overview

The problem

The client delivers food products to restaurants across Southern Ontario from a warehouse of over 2,000 items. Returns were handled on paper. Products could come back without anyone knowing which customer they came from, there was no check that deliveries reached the right customer, and the existing system purged its data at the end of every month.

Step 1

Plan the project

I wrote the project charter: background, business case, scope, exclusions, stakeholders, roles and a week-by-week milestone plan. The agreed scope was to track where products are delivered, notify staff about wrong deliveries and return requests, and record which truck carries which items.

Step 2

Model the process

We met with the client to walk through the warehouse and the returns process, then documented five processes: return a product, replace a product, create a returns form, ship items and issue a credit. Each one got a use case model, a detailed use case description (actors, triggers, preconditions, flow and exceptions) and a CRUD analysis.

From there we drew as-is and to-be BPMN diagrams for all five processes, a diagnostic of causes, problems and consequences, an ERD and database schema, a class diagram, and a sequence diagram for each use case.

Step 3

Design the solution

We weighed a few options, including tablets with a simple Microsoft form and process changes such as clearer ownership between the warehouse coordinator and manager. Our first prototype was a multi-page Google Form covering order details, customer, reason for return and a product photo.

Because the client already used Microsoft tools, we rebuilt it as an app in Microsoft Power Apps with two forms:

  • Driver form: the driver photographs the returned product, the photo uploads to the company's SharePoint, and the right employees are notified.
  • Employee form: warehouse staff search for a return or replacement, record the reason and other details, and send it for approval.

A Power Automate flow notifies the coordinator, who approves or denies each request.

Step 4

Plan the rollout and the risks

I co-owned the implementation and deployment plan with two teammates. It broke the rollout into nine tasks with dates and status: build the driver photo form, build the employee return form, set up the notification flow, create driver accounts, set up each phone and test an upload, train the drivers, train the warehouse staff and coordinator, run a one-month trial, then review the system.

The other half of the team built a risk register alongside it. It rated seven risks by likelihood and impact and gave each one an owner and a mitigation. The highest was data security, because the return form holds customer information. Others included poor data entry, integration with the client's existing systems, drivers forgetting to photograph returns, and drivers finding the app hard to use.

Step 5

Build and hand over

The app and its landing pages were finished on March 29, 2023, and the notification automation on April 4. The rollout plan handed the next steps to the client: driver accounts and phone setup, training for drivers, warehouse staff and the coordinator, a one-month trial, then a review of the system and its risks.

Results

Outcome and what I learned

Everything came together in a final report of more than 90 pages and a final presentation. The client received a working app and automation that put all return information in one place, with a photo for every return. The trial and review were scheduled to run after the course ended, so there are no measured results to report.

What I learned

  • How to lead a team project: a charter and weekly milestones kept six people working toward the same deliverables.
  • Start from the people doing the work. Drivers, warehouse staff and the coordinator each needed a different screen.
  • A low-code tool can replace a paper process quickly when the requirements are clear.
  • A project isn't finished at handover. Training and a trial period have to be planned, not assumed.
